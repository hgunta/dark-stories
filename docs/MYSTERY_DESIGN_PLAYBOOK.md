# Dark Stories — Mystery Design Playbook (v1)

**Status:** Editorial guide, not game content or a substitute for blind player testing.
**Companion study:** [REFERENCE_MYSTERY_STUDY.md](./REFERENCE_MYSTERY_STUDY.md).
**Latest previous failed experiment:** [PREMISE_FIRST_EXPERIMENT_RESULTS.md](./PREMISE_FIRST_EXPERIMENT_RESULTS.md).

## Our creative north star

> **“I didn't think of that, but now every clue makes sense — and the
> questions I asked helped me see it.”**

The game sells an enjoyable **investigation and satisfying reinterpretation**.
Discomfort, suspense, odd humor and tragedy may enrich that experience,
but they are not substitutes for it.

## What the classics teach — and what we will *not* copy

1. **Contradictory behavior with a hidden rational purpose** often sustains
   discussion better than a hidden device. The bar/water classic uses the
   apparent aggression *and* gratitude as mutually constraining observations.
2. **Several details explained by one cause** can be genuinely elegant. The
   elevator/rain example works because the weather exception confirms the
   hidden constraint. We do not need exactly two plot twists.
3. **A small present fact may force reinterpretation of the past**, creating
   emotion as a *consequence of deduction*. Albatross soup illustrates this
   potential while also warning against arbitrary unforeshadowed tragedy.
4. **One-word category answers** can make good warmups but usually poor premium
   cases. Fish, parachute and Monopoly belong in the “quick classic” category,
   not the benchmark for new 6–10 minute experiences.
5. **Not every classic is airtight.** A fixed answer is not necessarily the
   only reasonable answer; wording, plausibility and fair alternative theories
   need active scrutiny.

See the reference matrix for fourteen named examples, sources and limitations.
**Never adapt their actual plots, distinctive objects or scene combinations.**

## Three non-negotiable tests

### A. The backwards-click test

Read the full answer, then every public premise clause in reverse order.
Can the reader point to a *specific causal reason* why each odd detail was
present? Remove any detail that exists solely to delay guessing or create
atmosphere unless atmosphere has a clear game purpose.

### B. The fair-branch test

A typical player's first broad questions should split plausible mechanisms:
- Was the behavior intentional or accidental?
- Is the protagonist's assumed goal correct?
- Did the clue exist before or after the event?
- Does another person interpret the same event differently?
- Is the observer's report direct evidence or inference?

At least **two substantively different investigative directions** should
produce informative answers (Yes / No / Irrelevant, according to what the
case establishes). No arbitrary hidden password fact.

### C. The rival-solution test

Write the two strongest *alternative* explanations that satisfy the premise.
For each, find the **actual observation** that excludes it. If none does,
either enrich the public facts fairly or allow the alternative to count as a
legitimate solution in gameplay. **Never declare a truthful theory “No” merely
because it differs from a canonical story.**

## Quality of a *single* twist versus “two twists”

The old “two meaningful revelations” requirement is **withdrawn as a hard
rule**. A single, unusually elegant inference may be worth shipping if blind
players find the investigation enjoyable; two unrelated twists may make a
case worse. Instead:

- **Core inference:** What natural assumption must be overturned?
- **Independent confirmation:** Which *different public detail* becomes
  comprehensible under the same explanation?
- **Consequential payoff:** What changes in the meaning of the action or
  outcome when the inference is made?

A candidate is weak if its three answers are just paraphrases of “it was
a recording,” “it was recycled fabric,” or “the room moved.”

## Two sizes of cases, not a forced uniform recipe

| Type | Experience | Editorial expectation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Quick spark** | 2–4 minute surprising reinterpretation | Extremely fair, one beautiful clue, no added complexity to make it last |
| **Feature mystery** | 5–10 minute investigation (initial hypothesis; measure against players) | Multiple grounded clues and question routes, a memorable consequential reveal |

These are **test targets**, not enforced durations. Don't manipulate the
moderator into suppressing true answers to meet the clock.

## Five failure modes to reject early

1. **Retrofitted tragedy.** The premise is solved by one simple fact, then an
   invented murder/family loss is appended to seem dark.
2. **One-noun technical answer.** A named gadget, malfunction, job title or
   unusual physical state ends all meaningful questioning.
3. **Author-controlled behavior.** A character makes a highly implausible
   decision solely to guarantee a tragic or ironic ending.
4. **Premise truth drift.** “The window broke” secretly means “an existing
   broken pane was removed,” or a witness inference is presented as fact.
5. **Moderator as obstacle.** Players reasonably find the explanation, but
   the host says No or continues asking for canonical trivia.

## Commissioning process — evidence, not artificial requirements

**Phase 1 — Original seed.** Commission five **mechanically diverse** ideas.
For each, limit initial output to a title, 2–3 sentence public setup, 80–120
word causal solution, central mistaken assumption, and its strongest alternate
explanation. The premise and solution are internal drafts.

**Phase 2 — Blind screen.** Show only the public premise to two independent
cold readers (ideally humans; different AI contexts are merely proxies).
Ask for their immediate full explanations and first three Yes/No questions.
If the intended solution is guessed instantly, decide whether it's a
**good quick spark** or not worth development.

**Phase 3 — Adversarial audit.** Independently examine physical feasibility,
literal truth, rival answers, special-knowledge dependencies, manipulated
human motivation and whether the payoff is causally earned. Do not revise a
dead premise with extra secret facts; replace it.

**Phase 4 — Moderator goldens.** For each surviving case write: canonical
solution; minimal acceptable theory; several equivalent alternative theories;
exact true/false/ambiguous questions; near-solve examples; and two graduated,
nonspoiling hints. Test against the *actual* Luna moderator and private guess
endpoint, not just isolated textual judgment.

**Phase 5 — Five human blind sessions.** Obtain at least five *first-time*
sessions per candidate, with players who never saw the answers. Measure
satisfaction (1–5), perceived fairness, meaningful investigative discoveries,
time to solve, moderator false negatives, spoilers, and desire for another
case. Favor player feedback over generated scoring. The initial proposed
targets are reveal satisfaction >=4/5 median, perceived fairness >=80%, no
verified moderator false No on goldens, and >=70% wanting another case;
these are provisional experiments, not validated norms.

**Phase 6 — Owner approval.** Only **two or three** strong cases may be
submitted to a separate preview content branch after editorial review.
Never auto-merge or auto-publish. Production remains protected.

## Case-specific scorecard (0–5), avoiding score inflation

| Attribute | 5 means | Zero/one means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hook | Crisp, vivid apparent contradiction | Generic puzzlement or jargon |
| Reinterpretation | Several observed details take on new meaning | A trivia noun or arbitrary explanation |
| Logic and inevitability | Every claimed fact and crucial human decision is plausible | Wording sleight or plot-serving coincidence |
| Discoverability | Questions naturally reveal meaningful constraints | Unstated password facts or luck |
| Payoff | Distinctive human, ironic or unsettling implication earned by deductions | Decoration, unearned suffering, or mundane maintenance |

**Use scores as diagnostics**, never an automatic green light. No story
qualifies with a material logical flaw; even a strong average can hide one.

## Moderator and win-state contract

- **True partial hypotheses receive Yes.** “Are there two fish?” cannot be
  denied merely because the preferred answer adds a species.
- A near-complete theory earns clear acknowledgement and a prominent,
  understandable way to submit a final explanation.
- **Canonical keywords are not passwords.** Any causally equivalent theory
  that explains the strange observation deserves credit.
- The story-specific moderator must not volunteer the answer as a hint.
- A full solution should trigger a clear win state through the actual
  final-theory submission path; a positive Yes alone should not pretend
  the game is complete.

## Ready-to-use brief for five new cases

> Create **five original** situation mysteries inspired by the design
> principles of concise contradiction, misread intent, converging clues,
> and earned reinterpretation — **not by copying any classic mechanism or
> our previously rejected stories**. Use different emotional registers
> (eerie, darkly comic, ironic, unsettling, poignant where warranted).
> For each, give a 2–3 sentence public premise, coherent short solution,
> hidden assumption, two public details that become meaningful under
> the answer, three useful first questions, two plausible rival solutions,
> and why the proposed answer beats those rivals. Don't pad with
> additional hidden facts, specialist equipment or unmotivated deaths.
> It is acceptable to return **fewer than five** if the rest are weak.
> Label all work **unapproved editorial candidates**.

## Stop rule

**If three consecutive batches fail human or independent blind review,
stop automated generation and re-examine the creative brief and audience
feedback.** We have already spent effort on over-structured prompts; adding
more checkboxes is not a guarantee of better stories.
