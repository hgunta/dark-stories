## S01 — The Fifth Name

**Public premise (share only this with blind players):**

> After an apartment fire, officials said all five residents had been accounted for. One of the four people standing safely outside crossed her own name off the list. The firefighters immediately began searching for a missing person.

**Private canonical solution (spoiler):**

The list had been assembled quickly from two sets of resident records and accidentally included the same woman twice, under her current surname and her former surname. Both entries had been marked safe after she gave her name. There were **five different people** living in the building but only **four distinct people** accounted for. She recognized the duplicate, crossed out one of her two entries, and forced the team to correct the head count. The firefighters resumed searching for the fifth resident; the woman wasn't undoing her own rescue or inventing another person.

**Assumption overturned:** Five checked names necessarily mean five distinct people have been accounted for.

**Two independently useful paths:** Verify the difference between a **name** and a **person**; compare the resident roster with the people physically outside.

**Minimum accepted answer:** “She was counted twice under different names, so another real resident was still unaccounted for.” Exact surnames are not a password.

**Likely true partials:** “Was someone counted twice?” Yes. “Was she already safe?” Yes. “Were five names necessarily five people?” No. “Was someone else still missing?” Yes.

**Strongest rival:** An official simply counted incorrectly or had a faulty resident roster. Neither uniquely contradicted by the original premise; the case must accept reasonable equivalent double-counting explanations rather than falsely denying them.

**Editorial concern:** This is a compact counting/identity correction, not a 10-minute mystery. The words “four people” and “five residents” point strongly to its solution. Test as a *quick spark*, and ask whether that early discovery is genuinely enjoyable.

## S02 — The Rehearsal Note

**Public premise:**

> A theater director received a threatening note that described exactly where a heavy stage prop would fall during the next performance. She canceled the show and thanked the person who delivered it. When the stage crew checked, the prop was securely fastened, and the note had been written by the director herself.

**Private canonical solution (spoiler):**

The threatening appearance of the note was misleading: it was an old safety instruction the director had written for a **previous** rehearsal, identifying where a prop could fall if it were rigged incorrectly. A stagehand found the note among rehearsal materials and delivered it without its original heading, thinking it was a current warning. The director canceled the performance because the note exposed something genuinely alarming: the fastening arrangement had changed since the rehearsal, and the crew was relying on an older checklist rather than confirming the revised setup. The prop was secure when inspected, but she needed the rigging process and written sign-off reconciled before spectators returned. She thanked the messenger for surfacing a gap, not for predicting an accident.

**Assumption overturned:** A note describing a future accident must be a deliberate current threat; a prop being fastened correctly now proves the safety process is sound.

**Two investigative paths:** The **chronology and original purpose** of the note; the **difference between current physical safety and reliable safety checks**.

**Minimum accepted answer:** “It was her own earlier safety warning rediscovered out of context; she canceled to verify the changed rigging and procedure, not because someone threatened to drop the prop.”

**Likely true partials:** “Was it originally a threat?” No. “Was it written before the current performance was planned?” Yes. “Was the director worried about a safety process?” Yes. “Was anyone deliberately sabotaging it?” No.

**Strongest rival:** A director wrote a staged threat to test her team. The premise does not rule this out. Also, the solution's procedural failure may feel like invented backstage facts. **This is only a feature *prospect* and should probably be replaced if cold readers find the motivation or stage logistics contrived.** The word “threatening” must be explicitly attributed to how the note appeared, not asserted as the note's intent.

## S03 — The Open Window

**Public premise:**

> After a storm, a gallery owner found a window wide open and a small sculpture missing. The tabletop directly under the window was dry, even though rain had blown hard against that wall all night. The owner believed the window had been opened to mislead investigators.

**Private proposed solution (spoiler):**

The owner inferred the window had been opened after the rain ended, because the furniture directly below it showed no windblown moisture. An employee with keys had earlier removed the sculpture, then opened the window as a false entry route after the storm passed. The open window was a staged clue rather than the real exit.

**Assumption overturned:** The most conspicuous open route must be the route the thief used.

**Useful paths:** When was the window opened relative to the rain? Who had ordinary access to the gallery?

**Strongest rival / fatal weakness:** An outside burglar could have entered through that window **after the storm ended**. The dry tabletop does not exclude this and therefore does not justify the specific employee accusation. **Hold / rework; do not playtest this as a unique-answer mystery.**

## S04 — The Wrong Photograph

**Public premise:**

> A photographer showed a woman a picture of a room destroyed in a fire. The woman knew the picture had been developed after the fire and yet it showed the room intact. She did not accuse the photographer of lying; instead, she asked for the original negative.

**Private proposed solution (spoiler):**

The negative had been exposed before the blaze but developed afterward. The woman wanted the negative to examine whether the original capture date and scene could be independently corroborated; a late-developed print does not establish that a camera observed the room after it was destroyed.

**Assumption overturned:** Processing/printing and capturing a photograph are the same event.

**Useful paths:** Exposure versus development; why the person wanted original evidence.

**Strongest rival / weakness:** The woman could simply know that photographs can be developed later, with no further puzzle. Asking for the negative is routine verification, not a surprising human consequence. The distinguishing mechanism appears in the earliest natural question. **Hold / rework; likely inferior to an honest 30-second warm-up.**

## S05 — The Donation That Stayed

**Public premise:**

> During a charity auction, a man bid repeatedly for the same inexpensive object even after winning it. He handed it back each time and became increasingly angry when the auctioneer finally refused his next bid.

**Private proposed solution (spoiler):**

He was trying to raise a public funding total by buying and re-donating the object. He grew angry because the auctioneer had learned his donations were going to a cause he opposed and stopped accepting them.

**Assumption overturned:** Bidding after purchasing an object is an attempt to acquire it again, rather than to donate more.

**Strongest rival / fatal weakness:** Reauctioning an object for charity is an obvious opening guess, and the auctioneer's objection is a completely unforeshadowed political/moral disagreement. It adds a second reveal by fiat, not deduction. **Reject.**

## First-pass editorial decision

These five are **not equivalent in strength** and **none is publication-ready**.

- **S01** is the clearest legitimate quick-spark premise: it has a concrete mistaken assumption, a verifiable causal answer, and a consequential human action. Its simplicity should be evaluated openly, not disguised by moderator evasiveness.
- **S02** has the strongest *potential* for a richer investigation, but the human behavior and rigging details remain weak. This is a **conditional** candidate: a blind reader should be allowed to reject it immediately if the solution feels retrospectively invented.
- **S03–S05** are documented failures; keeping them in the slate prevents the same mistakes returning as “new” concepts next time.

**No independent blind screen has occurred. No Sol / Astra scores have been fabricated. No moderator test has occurred.**
