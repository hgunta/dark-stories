# Dark Stories — Reference Mystery Study

**Date:** November 2026 · **Purpose:** Derive original creative principles from established situation-puzzle examples, not reproduce or import their text. **Editorial research only.**

## Method and evidence limits

Studied fourteen commonly published situation-puzzle archetypes from multiple published collections, including
[Post Mortem's classic index](https://laboitebleue.games/en/post-mortem/riddles/classics/),
[PlayWhydunit's classic set](https://playwhydunit.com/en/guides/10-lateral-thinking-puzzles-with-answers/),
[its second set](https://playwhydunit.com/en/guides/10-more-classic-lateral-thinking-puzzles-answers/),
and book listings for
[Sloane / MacHale, *Great Lateral Thinking Puzzles*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Great_Lateral_Thinking_Puzzles.html?id=La84Qmo_Q1oC) and
[Sloane / MacHale / Harshman, *Classic Lateral Thinking Puzzles*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Classic_Lateral_Thinking_Puzzles.html?id=e--znfbMSckC).

The above are **documented examples of the genre**, not a statistically
representative top-rated list. No empirical completion/satisfaction results
were supplied by these sources. The strengths/weaknesses below are *our own
editorial judgments*, not measured outcomes or endorsements.

To avoid copying others' content, only short labels and **abstract mechanisms**
are recorded below. **Do not import these cases verbatim or generate thin
reskins.** Where multiple variants differ, the evaluation addresses the
representative common version.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Bar patron, water and gun | Apparent aggression can be a helpful intervention; the patron's goal was relief, not hydration | Three distinct observed behaviors all become rational at once | A guessed condition can collapse the plot quickly; don't force extra detail after player solves |
| 2 | Elevator rider on rainy days | A choice is actually a physical inability; an object available only on rainy days removes it | Multiple anomalies (floor choice, stairs, weather) cross-check one constraint | Stock physical-trait reveal; beware framing disability merely as a trick |
| 3 | Albatross soup | Present sensation is compared to a memory built on deception | A small present fact forces a devastating reinterpretation of past events | Hidden prior catastrophe could be arbitrary if no player question can surface it; suicide not required for sophistication |
| 4 | Romeo and Juliet | Familiar names create an unearned assumption about what kinds of beings died | Water, glass and setting snap into a coherent visual scene | One-word species switch; often too easy once asked “are they human?”; our game already uses fish |
| 5 | Unopened package in field | A “package” may be protective equipment; no ground approach is needed | Scene, location and failure all have one physical cause | One-noun parachute answer; our current library already has this trope |
| 6 | Hanging victim over puddle | The missing support can have changed physical state | Apparent missing evidence leaves a trace of its disappearance | Melting-ice trope reused too often; a single mechanical answer |
| 7 | Pushed car at hotel | Ordinary nouns may describe objects in a game rather than real-world objects | A complete shift of scale/context instantly reconciles all details | Pure category substitution (“Monopoly”), not a rich conversational case |
| 8 | Night watchman rewarded and fired | Helpful information and misconduct can coexist; source of knowledge implicates the informant | The happy and unhappy consequences genuinely derive from the same event | Requires the watcher to have slept on duty; some players may anticipate it immediately |
| 9 | Phone booth with outward glass | Unusual evidence records physical gesture rather than assault | Physical traces constrain the action; an everyday emotional gesture supplies motive | Severity of injury/death may strain plausibility, and old technology dates it |
| 10 | New shoes before fatal circus act | Tiny changed dimensions matter when a repeated precision task depends on them | One seemingly mundane clue has a mechanical consequence and consequential outcome | May require inventing a highly specific occupation; can be a one-fact occupational riddle |

| 12 | Music stops, performer dies | A signal means positional safety; premature stoppage produces a false belief | Player must connect a familiar cue with its unexpected use and then its failure | Exact stage setup may be arbitrary; medical/tragedy embellishment unnecessary |
| 13 | Broken match in desert | Small object encodes a group decision under emergency conditions | The ordinary object becomes evidence of a forced choice; severe consequences flow from it | Largely withheld catastrophe; several equally plausible balloon/jump stories |
| 14 | One-way ticket after cruise death | Apparently mundane prior purchase becomes evidence of foreknowledge | Evidence predating death changes interpretation of the event | A single travel-document clue can be insufficient for uniquely identifying a crime; real-world ticket policies vary |

## Five structural patterns (not five templates to copy)

**1. Purpose reversal:** A seemingly hostile/foolish action becomes rational
once the actor's actual goal is known. Strong when the opening includes both
the action and an independent reaction that corroborates the goal.

**2. Shared-cause compression:** Two or three strange *observations* turn out
to be consequences of one hidden constraint. Great in short cases when
the constraints cross-check; shallow if a single noun explains it all with no
investigation.

**3. Context / scale correction:** Players infer a physical or social
context from familiar language, then learn a different one. Works especially
well for a quick warm-up; not automatically suitable as a flagship case.

**4. Time / provenance inversion:** A trace that appears to be a cause,
intentional signal, or prediction is actually a delayed record or consequence.
The story must avoid moving the goalposts about what was witnessed.

**5. Recontextualizing evidence:** A detail that seemed insignificant or
benevolent becomes crucial evidence of a different history. Especially strong
when it changes a consequential decision the player had already heard about.

## Negative lessons from famous puzzles

- A **single correct insight can be excellent**. Never require exactly two
  discoveries. But one-step identification puzzles may be too slight for
  a conversational medium. Test satisfaction, not twist count.
- Some classics have **multiple plausible answers**. Treat “the canonical
  solution” as an author-selected story, not always a logical entailment.
  When alternatives fit all public facts, moderator must acknowledge or
  accept plausibly equivalent theories according to case rules.
- A red herring should misdirect an assumption, **not falsely state a fact**.
- Job/profession or vocabulary tricks grow stale; avoid specialist mechanisms
  and dated assumptions as the only point of interest.
- Death or tragedy alone does not make a payoff satisfying.
- Many of these are **widely recognizable**. Do not promote close variations
  as original Dark Stories content.

## Dark Stories product implication

The experience has a second author: the moderator. Even strong material is
ruined by No to a truthful partial deduction or a win state that never appears.
A new case needs explicit alternative acceptable explanations, a near-solve
threshold, and a clear final-theory action.

**Research conclusions are provisional qualitative inferences, not measured
player preferences.** The next step is a controlled blind playtest comparing
new original cases with one well-understood control.
