# Two-revelation commissioning slate — editorial v3

**Status: no-cost, human-authored exploration; all unapproved.** This is a
premise/answer viability exercise, not new playable game content.

## Rule before a new story gets a full draft

There must be **two distinct, causally connected deductions**, each
discoverable through a different family of truthful yes/no questions:

A. **What was apparently observed, and which assumption was false?**

B. **What did someone consequently do, or why did it matter?**

The second cannot merely be a recitation of how the first mechanism works, or
an irrelevant evil act attributed to an unnamed person.

For both A and B, document (1) a fair path of two questions, (2) a concrete
premise detail that makes the path relevant, and (3) the minimum complete
solution. Ask whether that complete solution is also the obvious first guess.
If so, reject even when it fits the story.

## Commissioned seed directions — before full writing

### Seed A — The Counted Cash (counterfeit/denomination reversal)

**Public premise:** After a break-in, a shopkeeper found *more banknotes*
in the till than he had left the previous evening. He still reported a cash
theft; the intruder had intentionally left the extra notes.

**Proposed causal solution:** The intruder replaced a smaller number of large
banknotes with a larger number of small, genuine banknotes. The number of
notes increased while their value decreased. The thief did it to make a
cursory check based on note-count miss the theft until the next reconciliation.

**First discovery:** Count of notes is not value of money.

**Second discovery:** The thief intentionally used the misleading count as
cover; the overfull till is part of the theft's concealment, not a donation.

**Red team:** The second point is merely the thief's obvious intent given
the swap; there is no independently discoverable second surprise. The premise
does not say the owner counted notes rather than money, and a shopkeeper
would naturally look at value. **REJECT as a premium case.**

### Seed B — The Stolen Pawn (ownership versus custody)

**Public premise:** Someone broke into a pawnshop, removed one ring and left
more cash than the shopkeeper had offered for it. The ring's previous owner
later demanded the police return the cash to the burglar.

**Proposed causal solution:** The owner had pawned the ring, but its sentimental
value to a family exceeded the offered loan and they could not legally
recover it without redeeming the loan. The burglar was the owner's relative,
who left what they believed was enough money to cover the debt; the owner
didn't want the relative to have paid for the ring at the cost of prosecution.

**First discovery:** The act is a desperate attempt to reclaim a pledged
object rather than to sell stolen jewelry.

**Second discovery:** The owner's demand concerns the burglar's sacrifice,
not the value of the ring.

**Red team:** The necessary pawn terms, relationship and motive are all
author-selected, not deducible from the premise. The original ring owner
requesting the return of money is also a legally irrelevant action. **REJECT.**

### Seed C — The Photograph After the Fire (time and material provenance)

**Public premise:** A photographer delivered a photograph of the inside of a
building after the building had burned down. She said she had never entered
the building, and the negatives were developed after the fire.

**Proposed causal solution:** The negative was exposed before the fire, then
developed and printed afterward. The photographer had used a long lens
through a window rather than entering.

**First discovery:** Developing a photograph and exposing its negative are
different events.

**Second discovery:** The image could be captured from outside.

**Red team:** Each discovery is a separate, truthful answer but the second
does not dramatically change the meaning of the first. Neither explains
why someone would care; both are obvious first guesses. **REJECT.**

### Seed D — The Buried Letter (different meanings of possession)

**Public premise:** A woman who had never visited her brother's grave
produced a letter that he had put inside his coffin. The cemetery confirmed
the coffin had not been opened.

**Proposed causal solution:** The brother had written duplicate letters,
putting one in his coffin and giving one to her before his death. She lied
about never visiting? No; she held the other copy.

**First discovery:** The letter she produced is not necessarily the physical
letter in the coffin.

**Second discovery:** The brother deliberately gave her the duplicate.

**Red team:** Duplicate letters are a cheap first answer; the reason for
creating them would be invented. This also risks the false impression that
the premise refers to the very same physical sheet. **REJECT.**

### Seed E — The Room That Paid Twice (mistaken identity in a record)

**Public premise:** A hotel refunded a guest for a room he had definitely
slept in. The manager also reported him for stealing the room's entire
contents, even though nothing was missing when housekeeping checked.

**Proposed causal solution:** The guest had entered the wrong room, which
belonged to someone who had silently moved out; the hotel refunded a charge
for another booked room. "Contents" were documents or data copied, not
physical objects.

**First discovery:** Paid-for room and occupied room are not the same.

**Second discovery:** Theft can involve copies rather than removal.

**Red team:** Two disjoint lateral shifts, not one coherent causal reveal.
Digital/data theft is a generic escape hatch, and room access logistics
depend on unprovided details. **REJECT.**

### Seed F — The Apology Before the Crime (false chronology)

**Public premise:** A handwritten apology naming the victim and describing
a particular injury was dated before an assault. Police established that
the writer had not predicted, ordered or committed the assault.

**Proposed causal solution:** The apology was written after a different
incident that caused the same type of injury; the later event was unrelated.

**First discovery:** The note concerns an earlier event.

**Second discovery:** The same injury happened again.

**Red team:** Pure coincidence with no linked motive or explanatory power.
In effect it changes which incident the clue refers to and then adds an
arbitrary repeated injury. **REJECT.**

## Outcome

**0 of 6 qualify for full story development.** This result is a useful
calibration: merely writing "revelation 1" and "revelation 2" does not create a
two-step mystery.

### More promising commissioning method

Commission only *premise structures* with both an evidence reversal and an
independently surprising human action that depends on that reversal. Before
writing the full story, **strip away all fictional backstory** and ask
whether the public observations still constrain both deductions.

For every future candidate, the editorial test must say:
- First 3 natural questions a player would ask
- Exact minimal acceptable complete answer
- The piece of premise evidence that points to discovery A
- The separate piece of evidence that points to discovery B
- Why discovery B follows from A causally but is not just A restated
- One equally plausible rival answer; if available, what clue fairly
  distinguishes it
- Whether a two-sentence reveal would be satisfying to someone who didn't solve

No story can be marked SHORTLIST until these fields are answered and
independently challenged. Sol's 0–25 and Astra's prose remain editorial
inputs, not acceptance criteria.
